## q02 (single)

**Question:** If an employee cannot predict when an FMLA-related absence will be needed, when should they notify the employer?

**Reference:** As soon as practicable under the facts and circumstances of the particular case.

**Agent answer:**

When the approximate timing of an FMLA-related absence is not foreseeable (unforeseeable leave), the employee must adhere to the following notice requirements:

* **Timing of Notice:** The employee must provide notice to the employer **as soon as practicable** under the facts and circumstances of the particular case (29 CFR § 825.303(a)). Generally, it should be practicable for the employee to provide notice within the time prescribed by the employer's usual and customary notice requirements applicable to such leave (29 CFR § 825.303(a), (c)). 
* **Employer Policies:** Absent unusual circumstances, the employee must comply with the employer's usual and customary notice and procedural requirements for requesting leave (such as calling a designated phone number or specific individual) (29 CFR § 825.303(c)). However, if an emergency prevents immediate compliance (such as requiring emergency medical treatment), the employee is not required to follow the call-in procedure until their condition is stabilized and they have access to and can use a phone (29 CFR § 825.303(a), (c)).
* **Spokesperson:** If the employee is unable to give notice personally, a spokesperson (e.g., a spouse, adult family member, or other responsible party) may give the notice on their behalf (29 CFR § 825.303(a)).

## q03 (single)

**Question:** After FMLA leave, what job must the employer generally return the employee to?

**Reference:** The same position the employee held when leave commenced, or to an equivalent position with equivalent benefits, pay, and other terms and conditions of employment.

**Agent answer:**

On return from FMLA leave, an employee is entitled to be returned to:
* The **same position** the employee held when the leave commenced; or
* An **equivalent position** with equivalent benefits, pay, and other terms and conditions of employment [29 CFR § 825.214]. 

An "equivalent position" is one that is virtually identical to the former position in terms of pay, benefits, working conditions (including privileges, perquisites, and status), and involves the same or substantially similar duties and responsibilities requiring substantially equivalent skill, effort, responsibility, and authority [29 CFR § 825.215(a)].

**Question:** If my boss lets me finish work after my shift even though they did not ask me to, does that time count as work?

**Reference:** Work not requested but suffered or permitted is work time.

**Agent answer:**

Yes, that time counts as working time.

Management has a duty to exercise control and ensure that unwanted work is not performed if they do not want to pay for it; an employer cannot simply sit back and accept the benefits of the work without compensating the employee for it [29 CFR § 785.13].

## q08 (single)

**Question:** When can the cost of employer-provided lodging be counted as part of an employee's wages?

**Reference:** The reasonable cost of board, lodging, or other facilities may be considered as part of the wage paid an employee only where customarily ‘furnished’ to the employee.

**Agent answer:**

Under the regulations in 29 CFR Part 531, the cost of employer-provided lodging can be counted as part of an employee's wages only when specific conditions are met:

1. **Customarily Furnished:** The lodging must be customarily furnished by the employer to the employees (29 CFR § 531.30, § 531.2). This requirement is satisfied if the lodging is furnished regularly by the employer to its employees, or if the same or similar lodging is customarily furnished by other employers in the same or similar trade, business, or occupation in the same or similar communities (29 CFR § 531.31). 
2. **Voluntary and Uncoerced Acceptance:** The employee must receive the benefit of the lodging, and their acceptance of it must be completely voluntary and uncoerced (29 CFR § 531.30).
3. **Not Excluded by Collective Bargaining:** The cost of the lodging must not be excluded from wages under a bona fide collective bargaining agreement (29 CFR § 531.2).
4. **Not Primarily for Employer's Benefit:** The lodging must not be found to be primarily for the benefit or convenience of the employer (29 CFR § 531.3(d)(1)).
5. **Not in Violation of Law:** The lodging must not be furnished in violation of any federal, state, or local law, ordinance, or prohibition (29 CFR § 531.31).
6. **Cost Limitations:** When counted as wages, the "reasonable cost" of the lodging cannot exceed the actual cost to the employer (which comprises the cost of operation and maintenance, adequate depreciation, and a reasonable allowance of not more than 5.5% for interest on the depreciated amount of capital invested, without including any profit) (29 CFR § 531.3(a)–(c)). Furthermore, if that computed cost is higher than the fair rental value, the fair rental value becomes the maximum reasonable cost (29 CFR § 531.3(c)). Alternatively, the "fair value" of the lodging determined by the Secretary of Labor may be used instead of actual cost (29 CFR § 531.2).

Under FMLA regulations, whether an employee has met the 1,250-hour eligibility requirement is determined using the principles for establishing compensable hours of work under the Fair Labor Standards Act (FLSA) (29 CFR § 825.110(c)(1)). 

Under the FLSA's principles incorporated into these rules:
* **"Suffered or permitted" work counts as hours worked:** Work not specifically requested by the employer, but "suffered or permitted" to be performed, counts as working time (29 CFR § 785.11).

The reason the employee continues to work is immaterial (29 CFR § 785.11).

**Question:** Can my employer require me to attend job training without counting it as work time?

**Reference:** Attendance at lectures, meetings, training programs and similar activities need not be counted as working time if attendance is outside of the employee’s regular working hours, attendance is in fact voluntary, the course, lecture, or meeting is not directly related to the employee’s job, and the employee does not perform any productive work; Attendance is not voluntary, of course, if it is required by the employer.

**Agent answer:**

Under the federal regulations, an employer can only exclude training time from hours worked if **all** of the following four criteria are met (29 CFR § 785.27):

1. **Outside regular hours:** Attendance is outside of your regular working hours [29 CFR § 785.27(a)].
2. **Voluntary:** Attendance is in fact voluntary [29 CFR § 785.27(b)]. Attendance is *not* voluntary if it is required by the employer, or if you are led to believe that your working conditions or continued employment would be adversely affected if you do not attend [29 CFR § 785.28].
3. **Not directly related:** The training is not directly related to your job [29 CFR § 785.27(c)]. Training is considered directly related if it is designed to help you handle your present job more effectively (as opposed to training you for a different job or a new/additional skill) [29 CFR § 785.29].
4. **No productive work:** You do not perform any productive work during the training [29 CFR § 785.27(d)].

If your employer **requires** you to attend the training, the attendance is considered involuntary [29 CFR § 785.28]. Therefore, if the training is required by your employer, it **must** be counted as work time.

**Question:** If a 10-minute rest break counts as work, what cash-wage floor applies per hour in a non-overtime workweek after facility deductions?

**Reference:** Rest periods of short duration, running from 5 minutes to about 20 minutes, must be counted as hours worked; a cash wage of at least the applicable minimum wage for each hour worked.

**Agent answer:**

Under the federal regulations:

**Agent answer:**

Under the federal regulations, employer-provided lodging can count toward wages after deductions in a non-overtime workweek, subject to specific requirements:

### Counting Toward Wages After Deductions in a Non-Overtime Week
* **Deductions allowed:** Deductions for lodging may be made in non-overtime workweeks even if they reduce the cash wage below the minimum wage, provided that the prices charged do not exceed the **“reasonable cost”** of the lodging. 
* **Profit limitation:** If lodging is furnished at a profit, the deductions in a non-overtime week are considered illegal only to the extent that the profit reduces the wage (including the reasonable cost of the facilities) below the required minimum wage.

---

### What Must Be True of the Lodging
For lodging to be considered part of wages and for its cost or deductions to be recognized, the following requirements must be met:

1. **Customarily Furnished:** The lodging must be customarily furnished by the employer to the employees.
2. **Voluntary and Uncoerced Acceptance:** The employee must receive the benefit of the lodging, and their acceptance of it must be **voluntary and uncoerced**.
3. **Reasonable Cost or Fair Value:** 
   * The cost included as part of wages cannot exceed the "reasonable cost" to the employer, which does **not include a profit** to the employer or any affiliated person.
   * "Reasonable cost" is defined as the cost of operation and maintenance, including adequate depreciation, plus a reasonable allowance (not more than 5½ percent) for interest on the depreciated amount of capital invested by the employer. However, if this total is more than the **fair rental value**, the fair rental value shall be the reasonable cost.
   * Alternatively, the Secretary of Labor may determine a "fair value" for lodging in defined classes of employees or areas, which may be used in lieu of the actual measure of cost.
4. **Not Excluded by Collective Bargaining:** The cost of lodging cannot be included as part of wages if it is excluded therefrom by a bona fide collective bargaining agreement.
5. **Not Primarily for Employer Convenience:** Facilities found to be primarily for the benefit or convenience of the employer will not be recognized as reasonable and may not be included in computing wages.

If an employee used four weeks beginning February 1, 2008, four weeks beginning June 1, 2008, and four weeks beginning December 1, 2008, the employee would not be entitled to any additional leave until February 1, 2009. However, beginning on February 1, 2009, the employee would again be eligible to take FMLA leave, recouping the right to take the leave in the same manner and amounts in which it was used in the previous year. Thus, the employee would recoup (and be entitled to use) one additional day of FMLA leave each day for four weeks, commencing February 1, 2009. The employee would also begin to recoup additional days beginning on June 1, 2009, and additional days beginning on December 1, 2009. Accordingly, employers using the rolling 12-month period may need to calculate whether the employee is entitled to take FMLA leave each time that leave is requested, and employees taking FMLA leave on such a basis may fall in and out of FMLA protection based on their FMLA usage in the prior 12 months. For example, in the example above, if the employee needs six weeks of leave for a serious health condition commencing February 1, 2009, only the first four weeks of the leave would be FMLA protected.
